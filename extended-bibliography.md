# Extended Bibliography — The Hardest Letters
### A resource for teachers, librarians, and curious readers

This expands on the "Note on Sources" printed in the booklet itself, with fuller citations and additional sources consulted during research and fact-checking, organized by topic to match the book's chapters.

---

## Primary and period sources

**Moxon, Joseph.** *Mechanick Exercises on the Whole Art of Printing.* London, 1683–84. The earliest detailed English-language account of the printing trade, written by a working printer — hand punchcutting, casting, composing, and presswork, described firsthand.

**Fournier, Pierre-Simon.** *Manuel Typographique.* 2 vols. Paris, 1764–66. The classic French technical manual on type founding, including detailed engraved plates of the hand mould, punches, matrices, and finishing tools (Volume 1, Plate VI, includes the "Dressoir," the squared jig used to true the foot of cast type).

**Diderot, Denis, and Jean le Rond d'Alembert, eds.** *Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers.* Paris, 1751–72. The plates on "Imprimerie" (printing) and related trades document period tools and workshop arrangements.

---

## Modern reference works on typefounding and printing history

**Carter, Harry.** *A View of Early Typography up to about 1600.* Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1969. The standard modern scholarly account of early type design and production; also the translator and annotator of Fournier's *Manuel Typographique* into English.

**Updike, Daniel Berkeley.** *Printing Types: Their History, Forms, and Use.* 2 vols. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1922. The foundational English-language survey of typeface history from Gutenberg forward, including Jenson, Garamond, and the Aldine press.

**Gaskell, Philip.** *A New Introduction to Bibliography.* Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1972. The standard reference on the physical construction of printed books — imposition schemes, formes, quarto and folio formats, paper, and presswork.

**Eisenstein, Elizabeth L.** *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change: Communications and Cultural Transformations in Early Modern Europe.* Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1979. The major scholarly treatment of printing's broader historical and cultural impact.

**Circuitousroot.com — "The Typefounder's Hand Mold" and related pages.** An extensive, carefully sourced online reference on hand-casting technology, including direct excerpts and page images from Fournier and other period sources. Useful for cross-checking specific technical claims (the function of the nick, the mechanics of the hand mould, the dressing/rubbing process) against primary documentation.

## Typeface history — Jenson, Garamond, Aldus and Griffo, and beyond

**Morison, Stanley.** Writings on typography and letterform history, including his own account of designing Times New Roman for *The Times* of London (1932), with draftsman Victor Lardent.

For Aldus Manutius and Francesco Griffo's roman and italic types (Venice, 1490s–1500s), general type-history references (Updike; Carter) remain the standard starting point; specialist bibliographic catalogs of incunabula (see below) document specific Aldine editions.

**On Times New Roman:** widely documented history of the 1929–32 commission from *The Times*, Stanley Morison's design brief, and Victor Lardent's draftsmanship — e.g., Morison's own account, "The Times and the Newspaper Press," in **Morison, Stanley. *Printing The Times, 1785–1953: Some Account of the Means of Production and Changes of Dress of the Newspaper.* London: Printing House Square, 1953**; and **Barker, Nicolas. *Stanley Morison.* London: Macmillan, 1972.**

**On Georgia:** Matthew Carter's 1993 design for Microsoft, created for legibility on low-resolution screens; widely documented in design-history sources, including Microsoft's own published design notes.

## Print-shop economics and Gutenberg

General historical accounts of Johannes Gutenberg's financing arrangement with Johann Fust, the 1455 lawsuit in which Fust won control of Gutenberg's equipment and much of the Bible print run, and the estimated print run and sale prices of the Gutenberg Bible (approximately 180 copies; roughly 40 guilders on paper, up to 75 on vellum) are widely documented across standard Gutenberg biographies and printing-history surveys — e.g., **Kapr, Albert. *Johann Gutenberg: The Man and His Invention.* Translated by Douglas Martin. Aldershot: Scolar Press, 1996.**

**On manuscript production for comparison:** general scholarship on scribal copying rates and materials (commonly cited figures include a practiced scribe producing roughly 2,500–3,000 words per day, and a large Bible requiring the skins of 200 or more animals for parchment) appears across manuscript-studies and book-history literature.

## The physical book as object — quarto imposition and format

**Gaskell**, cited above, remains the standard reference for imposition schemes (how pages are arranged on a sheet before folding) and format terminology (folio, quarto, octavo).

**The Morgan Library & Museum, incunable catalog records** (themorgan.org/incunables) — consulted directly for a specific example, PML 115, *Die Cronica van der hilliger stat van Coellen* (Cologne: Johann Koelhoff the Younger, 1499), a folio (not quarto) example, useful for confirming how line count and page dimensions scale with format.

---

## Compositor speed and the Linotype era

Historical figures on hand-composition speed (commonly cited in "ems per hour," with an average worker managing roughly 700–1,000 ems/hour and a full ten-hour day yielding approximately 7,000 ems) are documented across nineteenth- and twentieth-century printing-trade histories, including accounts of public typesetting competitions ("the Swifts") — e.g., **Rumble, Walker. *The Swifts: Printers in the Age of Typesetting Races.* Charlottesville: University of Virginia Press, 2003.**

**On Ottmar Mergenthaler and the Linotype** (patented 1884) and its keyboard layout: the "etaoin shrdlu" arrangement of the two leftmost keyboard columns, based on English letter frequency, and the associated newspaper-composing-room folklore, are documented in numerous printing-trade histories and in the *Oxford English Dictionary*'s entry for the phrase itself.

**On the Ludlow Typograph:** its origin as a failed attempt at a cheaper Linotype, and its eventual role as a dedicated large-display-type caster, is documented in period trade literature on twentieth-century typesetting technology.

**On the Paige Compositor:** the mechanically ambitious (approximately 18,000-part) typesetting machine backed financially by Mark Twain, and its commercial failure against the simpler Linotype, is documented in Twain biographies and printing-history surveys — e.g., **Chernow, Ron. *Mark Twain.* New York: Penguin Press, 2025.**

## A note on this bibliography's limits

Several claims in the booklet rest on figures widely repeated across multiple secondary and trade sources (compositor speed, type-metal density, average page character counts) rather than a single citable primary study — in those cases, the booklet says explicitly where the number is an estimate rather than a precise historical constant, and this bibliography reflects the same honesty rather than manufacturing false precision with invented citations.
